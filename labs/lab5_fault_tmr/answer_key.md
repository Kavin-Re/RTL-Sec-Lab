# Lab 5 answer key

1. Two flips defeat the vote only when they hit the same bit index in two different copies: 3 pairs of copies x W bit indices = 3W bad pairs, out of C(3W, 2) equally likely pairs: P = 3W / C(3W,2) = 2/(3W-1). For W=32: 2/95 = 0.0211; W=128: 2/383 = 0.0052. Larger W spreads the flips over more positions, so a collision is less likely (but a wider register also has more flips arriving if the fault rate is per bit).
2. DMR+parity can only *detect* two-copy disagreements and then falls back (key reload from NVM, counter waits for the next beacon), so a fault costs recovery slots: dcp desyncs on a counter-FSM fault half of the time (0.5), on every output-register fault (1.0, detected but one bad slot), and on 2 counter flips with p about 0.7. TMR corrects in the same cycle (p_desync = 0 for every single fault) and fails only on same-bit collisions. The 4-point area difference buys correction instead of detection plus a protocol fallback.
3. Common-mode faults (the same bit flipped in all three copies by a clock or supply disturbance, or a spatially wide upset) pass the voter unchanged (T6). Cheaper measures: physical separation or different clocks of the copies and a global sensor/alarm (detect, then reload from NVM). Also not modelled: faults in voters, in the SipHash datapath, and in the beacon logic; and TMR_state does not protect the datapath. A fault in the key register is persistent, which is why the watchdog or NVM reload matters.

Common mistakes: treating p_desync as packet-loss rate (it is the probability that the fault ever causes desynchronisation); comparing `tmr` with `none_nowd` instead of `none` for the cost-benefit; reading the proxy percentages as synthesis results; using the 32-bit formula for the key.
